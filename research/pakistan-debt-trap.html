Summary

Pakistan has asked the IMF for money for the 24th time in 76 years. That single fact is the result of a debt trap it has entered with China, paying 5 to 7 percent interest on tens of billions of dollars in loans while its IMF loan costs about 2 percent. It is also a byproduct of an extremely bad relationship with India, one it could have used instead for cheaper imports of essentials. Instead, an estimated $10 billion a year still moves between the two countries through back channels in Dubai and Singapore, at inflated prices Pakistani consumers pay for. On top of that, Pakistan is now fighting militants along its Afghanistan border, a group it built and funded itself. As a result, most of the money it does have goes to either debt servicing or defense, leaving almost nothing for actual development.

There are lessons for Pakistan here from two countries that hit rock bottom just as hard and came out the other side in a far stronger position: Indonesia and India. How they turned things around, what they did differently, and what it would actually take for Pakistan and countries like it to do the same, even from the worst possible starting position, is what this video covers.

Problem

Pakistan is currently in a $7 billion, 37-month IMF Extended Fund Facility agreed in 2024, paired with a Resilience and Sustainability Facility loan, its 24th program since 1950, an average of one new bailout roughly every three years.

Government debt is money a country borrows when tax revenue does not cover its spending while foreign exchange reserves are the dollars a central bank keeps on hand to pay for imports and foreign debt, and when those run too low, a country turns to the IMF, a 191-member institution that lends emergency dollars in exchange for reform conditions, usually tax hikes and subsidy cuts, often called austerity.

A debt trap is what happens when a country keeps needing new loans just to service old ones without ever fixing the root cause. So why does Pakistan keep landing back here? The reason is four-pronged, each reinforcing the others.

A toxic relationship with India

War has been a convenient way for Pakistan's military establishment to distract its public from internal failures, a pattern that goes back to the country's first decade. Most of the major India-Pakistan wars trace back to an action taken from Pakistan's side, from the 1947-48 war through 1965 and 1971.1

The two countries came closest to a real peace deal in 2001, under Prime Minister Vajpayee and President Musharraf, at the Agra Summit. However, talks broke down over how to even describe the Kashmir dispute in the final text. That collapse happened because of a decision made inside Pakistan's military establishment, not India's.2

Why the pattern keeps repeating: war is a good way for Pakistan to distract its public from internal issues

On May 9, 2023, Pakistanis attacked military installations during anti-government protests after Imran Khan's arrest, an all-time low for the military's public standing that its own establishment branded a "Black Day." Two years later, with the economy still in crisis and militants attacking from Afghanistan, Pakistan was again in conflict with India: the April 2025 Pahalgam attack, followed by India's Operation Sindoor strikes in May. Conveniently, the country rallied behind the military again, a Gallup Pakistan survey found 96 percent believed Pakistan won and 92 percent said their opinion of the military improved, and May 10 was renamed "The Day of Righteous Battle." Pakistan is still largely led by its military, the civilian prime minister has little real say over decisions like these.3

The direct cost of this relationship: official trade with India, once $2.41 billion a year, has collapsed to about $1.2 billion since Pakistan suspended it in 2019, while roughly $10 billion a year still moves between the two countries anyway, rerouted through Dubai and Singapore at a markup Pakistani consumers pay for. Pakistan's tariff on imported cars is 80 percent versus 60 percent in India, and normal trade would have meant cheaper cars, parts, healthcare, and education from next door instead of far more expensive alternatives shipped or flown in from much further away.4

The Afghanistan blowback

Pakistan built and sheltered the Afghan Taliban for decades as a strategic hedge to maintain influence in Afghanistan and counter Indian presence there. Since the Taliban retook Kabul in 2021, that relationship has flipped: Pakistan's own defense minister says 2,500 to 3,000 militants infiltrated from Afghan soil in the past year, and 2025 was the deadliest year for security and civilian deaths from this violence in over a decade in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, roughly 1,240 deaths, a 34 percent jump.5

The group Pakistan built as leverage against India is now a live security threat inside its own borders, one more thing competing for money that could otherwise go toward fixing the economy.

The 76-year loop and the China debt trap

Each IMF program buys short-term relief; the underlying pattern of low tax collection, heavy energy sector losses, and repeated overspending has not changed enough to stop the next crisis.6

The China-Pakistan Economic Corridor started as a $45 billion plan and has grown past $62 billion. Pakistan owes Chinese creditors over $25 billion directly tied to it, and roughly $40 billion in accumulated Chinese debt overall, about one-third of its total external debt of nearly $125 billion. Chinese commercial loans carry rates over 5 percent, often above 7 percent, versus about 2 percent on IMF lending, roughly $2 billion a year in interest alone on the Chinese debt stock before touching principal.7

In fiscal year 2023 alone, Pakistan's total external debt servicing came to $20.81 billion, of which $4.42 billion was interest. The mechanism in one sentence: Pakistan borrows more, often from China again, just to service the Chinese debt it already has.8

An economy that doesn't export anything

Pakistan exports about $32.7 billion a year, mostly low-value goods like textiles and rice, against $78.5 billion in imports, dominated by petroleum, meaning it depends on the outside world to eat and to power itself while barely selling anything back.9

Exports have fallen from 16 percent of GDP in the 1990s to about 10.4 percent in 2024, well below both Bangladesh and India, and the World Bank puts the resulting export gap at nearly $60 billion versus what a country at Pakistan's income level should be selling the world.10

China is also Pakistan's single largest source of imports, at $23.5 billion a year, the same country it owes a third of its external debt to, deepening the same dependency from a different direction.11

Where it all lands

Defense spending has fallen as a share of the federal budget, from around 20 percent a decade ago to about 14.5 percent in FY2025-26, roughly $9 billion. That is not a peace dividend, it is debt servicing crowding defense out of a shrinking pie. Debt servicing and defense together now consume almost the entire revenue the government collects, leaving development funded by more borrowing, and the cycle repeats.12

To be fair, Pakistan is not the only country stuck in a debt trap, Sri Lanka, Zambia, and Ghana have all defaulted this decade. But Pakistan is one of the very few that keeps getting bailed out anyway despite failing again and again, and the reason is blunt: it is a nuclear-armed country.13

Brookings scholar Bruce Riedel lays out the US calculation plainly: the real fear isn't Pakistan's government, it's what happens to Pakistan's roughly 170 nuclear warheads if the state fails and a jihadist movement inherits them, a scenario he calls potentially "the worst security threat since the end of the Cold War." Afghanistan already became a failed state and was taken over by the Taliban, a group Pakistan itself created and funded. Imagine that same outcome in a country that actually has nuclear weapons: global markets would panic and no one would know where those weapons end up or who they'd threaten.14

That is the entire problem in one sentence: Pakistan has taken advantage of that leverage instead of fixing the root causes, because for 76 years it has never actually had to.

CASE STUDY: Sri Lanka's 2022 collapse

Reserves fell 16 percent to $1.94 billion the month before Sri Lanka defaulted on its entire $51 billion external debt on April 12, 2022, its first ever sovereign default. Credit rating downgrades the year before had already locked it out of capital markets. The result: fuel and food shortages, blackouts, and mass protests that toppled the government within months.

The Revolution

Both case studies below go beyond "they paid the loan back." The loan repayment was a symptom of a much bigger set of reforms, that is the part usually left out.

Case Study 1: Indonesia, From the Biggest Bailout in Asia to a Top-20 Economy

During the 1997-98 Asian Financial Crisis, Indonesia's GDP fell around 14 percent in a single year, and it needed a rescue package worth nearly $50 billion: a $10 billion IMF Stand-By Arrangement, over $8 billion from multilateral lenders, and $18 billion more from bilateral donors, the largest IMF-linked bailout ever assembled for one country at the time.15

Step 1: Clean up the banks, decisively. Indonesia closed 16 insolvent banks in November 1997 instead of propping them up indefinitely, recapitalized the banks worth saving under real government oversight, and built out bankruptcy law and financial transparency rules so failing firms could not just keep borrowing.16

Step 2: Pay the debt off early, and stay off it. Bank Indonesia settled its final IMF obligation, SDR 2.15 billion, about $3.18 billion, in October 2006, four years ahead of the original 2010 deadline, backed by a strong balance of payments and reserves projected to exceed $39 billion by year end.17

Step 3: Build the economy so you never need it again (the part most retellings skip). Indonesia banned raw nickel ore exports starting in 2020, forcing processing to happen domestically instead of shipping raw material abroad for someone else to profit from. Nickel export value rose from 17 trillion to 510 trillion rupiah, roughly a 30-fold increase in nominal value.18

Foreign direct investment jumped 44.2 percent to $45.6 billion in a single year, 2021 to 2022, as processing plants were built inside the country.19

Indonesia's economy has expanded 32.1 percent since the full nickel export ban took effect in 2020, through 2024, and the country now produces 58 percent of the world's nickel supply, feeding global EV battery and stainless steel demand.20

In February 2025, Indonesia launched Danantara, a new sovereign wealth fund consolidating state-owned enterprise assets worth roughly $900 billion, explicitly modeled on Singapore's Temasek, to professionalize state companies instead of letting them bleed money the way loss-making state enterprises typically do in countries like Pakistan.21

Growth was sustained at an average of 5.8 percent a year from 2005 to 2011, peaking at 6.35 percent in 2007, all after the crisis, not despite dealing with it.22

Case Study 2: India, From Pledging Its Gold to the World's 4th Largest Economy

In 1991, India's forex reserves crashed to just $1 to $1.2 billion, barely 2 to 3 weeks of import cover.23

India secretly airlifted 67 tonnes of gold, 47 tonnes to the Bank of England and 20 tonnes to the Union Bank of Switzerland, to raise about $600 million in emergency collateral and avoid outright default in May 1991.24

Step 1: Deregulate fast. India dismantled the License Raj, the permit system that had required government approval for nearly any private industrial activity, devalued the currency, and opened previously closed sectors to foreign investment.25

Step 2: Build an export industry that did not exist before. Software and IT services exports, close to zero in 1991, reached $246.4 billion in FY26, with the wider tech sector projected to hit $350 billion, near 10 percent of GDP, in 2026.26

Step 3: Keep compounding for over three decades. From a country that pawned its gold reserves in 1991, India overtook Japan in December 2025 to become the world's 4th largest economy by GDP, and is targeting 3rd place, overtaking Germany, by 2028.27

The throughline: India did not just take an IMF check and wait it out. It deregulated, opened to foreign capital, and built an entirely new export sector from nothing. That is the difference between a bailout and a turnaround.

The Solution

None of these fix a debt trap alone. Indonesia and India both did multiple things at the same time and kept doing them for over a decade. A single loan or a single reform headline never fixes this on its own.

Fix the tax base, not just the budget

Broaden collection into historically undertaxed sectors like agriculture and real estate instead of squeezing the same salaried taxpayers every review cycle. Pakistani research estimates "elite capture," where politically connected groups avoid tax and absorb subsidies, costs the country roughly 6 percent of GDP in lost revenue and misallocated spending.28

Break the crisis-with-India-as-distraction cycle

Every documented spike in Pakistan's military approval this decade has followed conflict with India, not economic reform. Addressing the underlying civil-military imbalance so India tension stops functioning as a release valve for domestic anger is a precondition for the other five items actually sticking.29

Diversify away from expensive, opaque bilateral debt

Chinese commercial loans at 5 to 7 percent cost far more than multilateral financing at roughly 2 percent. Restructuring or refinancing this debt at better terms is a direct, quantifiable saving, potentially over $1 to 2 billion a year in interest alone on Pakistan's existing Chinese debt stock.30

Normalize trade with India

Reopening official trade could replace a large share of the estimated $10 billion a year already moving through expensive third-country workarounds, at a lower cost and without the markup, while also opening access to cheaper cars, parts, healthcare, and education instead of far more expensive alternatives further away.31

Privatize and restructure loss-making state enterprises, and go after FDI like Indonesia did

Indonesia's downstream nickel policy attracted a 44 percent jump in FDI in a single year by forcing value-add to happen domestically instead of exporting raw material cheap. Energy sector losses are one of the top conditions in nearly every Pakistani IMF review, for a reason.32

Build an export industry that does not depend on aid

India had close to zero software export industry in 1991. It is a $246 billion-plus industry now. It does not have to be IT specifically, it has to be something the country can sell to the world instead of importing everything and financing the gap with debt.33

Closing

Pakistan is on its 24th IMF program, paying more interest to China than it would pay the IMF, and has cut itself off from its cheapest trading partner, all while debt servicing and defense eat almost the entire government revenue.

A real part of how it got here is documented, not speculative: a pattern of crisis with India arriving at moments the military establishment's domestic standing needed defending, from 1948 through the 2025 clashes, each time diverting attention and money away from the economic reform the country actually needs.

It is not alone in the debt trap itself. Sri Lanka, Zambia, Ghana, Laos, and the Maldives show what happens when a debt spiral goes unaddressed long enough: default, and years of painful restructuring.

Indonesia and India show the other ending. Both hit rock bottom, both went to the IMF, and both used the moment to overhaul how their economies actually made money, not just how they borrowed it.

The fixes are known, and mostly already written into Pakistan's own IMF program documents. The missing piece is following through on all of them at once, not just enough to get to the next tranche.

Early Warning Signs, for Any Country

Import cover under 3 months: if foreign exchange reserves cannot cover 3 months of imports, a country is in the danger zone. Sri Lanka's reserves were already collapsing months before its 2022 default.34

External debt-to-GDP above your country's threshold: under the joint IMF-World Bank Debt Sustainability Framework, the sustainable ceiling is about 30 percent of GDP for weaker economies, 40 percent for medium performers, and 55 percent for strong performers. Breaching your category's threshold is a formal early warning signal.35

Debt service eating too much of exports or revenue: the same framework flags debt service above roughly 15 to 21 percent of exports or 14 to 23 percent of government revenue, depending on a country's capacity category, as a warning that little money is left for anything but interest payments.36

Credit rating downgrades that cut off market access: this forces reliance on a shrinking pool of lenders, often on worse terms, exactly what happened to Sri Lanka the year before its default.37

A rising share of debt owed to one opaque bilateral lender: rather than diversified, transparent multilateral or market debt. Opaque terms make restructuring far harder later, as seen across Djibouti, Angola, Laos, the Maldives, and Pakistan.38

Lesson for struggling economies: address these signs before a crisis, not after. Restructuring after a default costs years of growth and real public suffering, as Sri Lanka's blackouts and shortages showed.

Lesson for well-performing economies: the same signs apply in reverse. A country with healthy reserves and diversified debt today can still drift into this pattern if defense spending or opaque borrowing quietly creeps up over a decade, exactly the pattern Pakistan's own history shows.
