Summary

On August 26, 2026, a glacier collapsed high in the Himalayas above Nepal's Rasuwa district, sending an avalanche of ice, rock, and debris into the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli river system. The resulting flood has killed more than 1,300 people, left over 5,000 more missing, and destroyed roughly a fifth of Nepal's installed hydropower and solar capacity in a single morning.1

Nepal's government has responded on two fronts at once. Internationally, it has shifted its diplomatic language from asking for aid to demanding compensation, formally naming India, China, and the United States as major industrial emitters responsible for climate damages, with Prime Minister Balendra Shah scheduled to press the case at the United Nations General Assembly on September 24.2 Domestically, President Ramchandra Paudel has struck a different note, telling the country during a national day of mourning that "this is not a time for division, nor is it a time to blame one another."3 This report follows the president's framing rather than the diplomatic one. Who owes Nepal what, and why, is a real question this channel will take on separately. What follows instead is narrower: given that a disaster like this was always going to hit a country like Nepal eventually, what should have been in place already, and what should Nepal build now, regardless of how the compensation question resolves.

The arithmetic is stark. Reconstruction will cost roughly Rs 723 billion, about $4.7 billion, more than a tenth of Nepal's entire economy.4 Nepal already has the same kind of pre-arranged financing facility the Philippines pioneered for exactly this kind of disaster, but Nepal's version is capped at $150 million, barely enough to matter against a $4.7 billion bill. The rest depends on money that has been pledged but not delivered. This report covers why Nepal was this exposed, what the Philippines has actually built with the same World Bank instrument over the past fifteen years, and what Bhutan has already proven works to prevent this kind of disaster rather than just pay for it afterward.

Problem

The Issue

On the morning of August 26, 2026, a section of glacier and bedrock high on the slopes near Langtang Lirung, on the Nepal-Tibet border, broke away and fell roughly 1,200 meters down a steep slope into the Lhende Khola, a tributary of the Bhote Koshi river.5 The avalanche briefly dammed the river before bursting through minutes later, sending a surge of water, mud, ice, and rock down the narrow Himalayan gorge. Water levels at Galchhi on the Trishuli River rose by roughly nine meters in about thirty minutes, a rate of increase consistent with a sudden upstream release rather than rainfall runoff.6 The U.S. Geological Survey initially registered the event as a magnitude 4.4 earthquake before reclassifying it as a magnitude 5.2 landslide, confirming that the shaking came from the mass movement itself rather than a seismic trigger.7 Nepal's own Department of Hydrology and Meteorology confirmed there had been no heavy rainfall in the area beforehand, ruling out a conventional monsoon flood as the cause.8

This was not a one-off event. It was the second major flood in fourteen months on the same river tributary: a July 2025 flood on the Bhote Koshi, caused by the rapid drainage of a supraglacial lake, washed away the Nepal-China friendship bridge and killed at least nine people.9 A separate glacial lake outburst flood struck the remote village of Tilgau in Humla district in May 2025.10 A subsequent scientific assessment found that the glacier that collapsed on August 26 had shown visible signs of instability in the days beforehand, and that an effective early warning system could have saved hundreds of lives.11

None of this was invisible risk. Nepal's hydropower buildout, one of the country's few real growth industries, sits concentrated in the same narrow, glacier-fed river gorges that produce the best hydropower sites in the country. A separate spell of heavy rainfall and landslides in 2025 had already impacted 32 hydropower projects across ten districts, including 17 operating plants totaling 180 megawatts and 15 more under construction totaling 338 megawatts.12 Nepal has recorded more than 26 glacial lake outburst floods since 1977, and scientists at the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development have already identified roughly 200 more glacial lakes across the wider Himalaya that pose a comparable outburst risk.13 Nepal was not caught by surprise by the existence of this risk. It was caught without a financing and prevention plan sized to match a risk it already knew about.

Context

As of September 12, 2026, Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority had confirmed 1,386 deaths and listed 5,130 people as still missing, with only 104 of the recovered bodies formally identified and returned to families.14 More than 13,700 people have been rescued, and roughly 600 foreign nationals from dozens of countries were reported unaccounted for in the early days of the disaster.15

The flood destroyed 33 motorable bridges and damaged four more, and destroyed 53 of the 64 suspension bridges in the affected area, cutting off entire communities.16 The energy sector was hit especially hard: thirteen hydropower projects with a combined capacity of 759 megawatts and five solar plants totaling 24 megawatts were damaged or destroyed.17 At the peak of the crisis, the Independent Power Producers' Association, Nepal reported that 917 people connected to eleven hydropower projects in the affected river corridors remained out of contact, with rescue teams working to determine whether they were trapped inside hydropower tunnels.18

The flood struck hardest across five districts: Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading, Gorkha, and Chitwan, plus Tanahun and both Nawalparasi districts further downstream where many bodies were eventually recovered.19 Bidur Municipality in Nuwakot recorded the highest number of damaged private homes of any local unit, with 3,026 houses affected.20

Nepal's estimated $4.7 billion reconstruction bill is more than 40 times what the country earned from electricity exports in the last fiscal year, and the flood destroyed roughly a fifth of the installed hydropower and solar capacity behind that entire export industry in a single morning.22

CASE STUDY: A Community Rebuilt Once, Then Lost Again

After the 2015 earthquake, hundreds of displaced families in Rasuwa resettled along the banks of the Trishuli River, the flattest and most accessible land available to them at the time. On August 26, 2026, that same community, roughly 250 households, was swept away in its entirety by the flood.23 It is a small but exact illustration of the report's central problem: rebuilding fast is not the same as rebuilding somewhere safe.

The Climate Responsibility Claim

It is scientifically established that human-driven warming is destabilizing the kind of high-altitude ice that collapsed above Rasuwa, and the pattern of repeated glacial failures on the same river system over the past three years is consistent with that broader trend.24 Nepal's government has drawn a direct line from that science to a claim for money: its Ministry of Finance and the Environment Ministry have jointly petitioned the United Nations' Loss and Damage Fund for an initial $20 million, and a separate formal compensation letter has gone to India, China, and the United States, describing them as major industrial emitters and reframing the request from charity to what Foreign Minister Shisir Khanal calls "legal and moral liability."25

Nepal's government did not invent the idea that the biggest emitters owe something to the smallest ones. On a per-person basis, the average American emits about 13.6 tonnes of carbon dioxide a year and the average Chinese resident about 9.1 tonnes, against roughly 2.2 tonnes for the average person in India, even though China and the United States are, respectively, the largest and second-largest total emitters in the world.26 That gap is exactly what the 2015 Paris Agreement was written to address: under Article 9, developed countries agreed to provide financial resources to help developing countries both cut emissions and adapt to a warming climate they did far less to cause.27 In practice, that promise has arrived late and thin. The original pledge of $100 billion a year, first made in 2009, was not actually met until 2022, two years past its original deadline, and more than half of what reaches the poorest countries still arrives as loans rather than grants; the least developed countries, as a group, now spend roughly twice as much servicing climate-related debt as they receive in climate finance.28 The successor goal, agreed at COP29 in November 2024, commits developed countries to $300 billion a year by 2035, a figure the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development says falls well short of the roughly $900 billion a year in outside finance that developing countries are estimated to actually need.29

The underlying record on China, India, and the United States is genuinely mixed, not a simple story of inaction. China added more wind and solar capacity in 2025 than the rest of the world combined, and its power sector emissions actually fell as renewables reached 40 percent of its generation mix, up from 37 percent the year before.30 India logged a record $150 billion in total energy investment in 2025, two-thirds of it in clean energy, and its own power sector emissions fell for the first time outside a recession.31 Globally, renewables overtook coal in electricity generation for the first time in 2025.32 And yet global energy-related emissions still rose 1.1 percent that same year, because total energy demand, pulled up by data centers, artificial intelligence, electric vehicles, and continued industrial growth, is still climbing faster than clean power can displace fossil fuels. The United States was the single largest driver of that increase, responsible for roughly a third of the entire global rise on its own, as American power producers reversed course and burned 10 percent more coal amid higher natural gas prices.33

No single country caused the glacier collapse above Rasuwa, and no single check will make Nepal whole. What the data actually supports is narrower than either side of that argument wants: global warming is real and measurable, the countries with the most capacity to cut emissions and fund adaptation elsewhere are still not doing enough of either, and Nepal is one of the countries paying the price first regardless of how that responsibility is eventually divided. A fuller accounting of where the emissions gap sits, and what each major economy owes because of it, is worth its own report. This one sets that debate aside deliberately, in the same spirit as President Paudel's own call for unity over blame. Whatever the eventual verdict on compensation, Nepal has to decide today how it prepares for the next glacier collapse, and that is the question this report actually answers.

The Financing Gap, Explained

A Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Option, or Cat DDO, is a line of credit that the World Bank pre-arranges with a government years before a disaster happens. Once that government formally declares a state of calamity, it can draw the money down within days, rather than starting from zero to negotiate emergency financing while a crisis is still unfolding. Nepal signed its own Cat DDO with the World Bank in March 2020, starting at $50 million, and increased it to $150 million in October 2024, less than two years before the Bhote Koshi flood.34 Against a country whose GDP was estimated at roughly $43 billion in 2024,35 even the absolute maximum the World Bank's own rules would allow Nepal to draw, 0.5 percent of GDP or $1 billion, whichever is lower, works out to roughly $215 million.36 Either number, the actual $150 million facility or the theoretical $215 million ceiling, is under 5 percent of the $4.7 billion reconstruction bill. This facility was never going to be the answer on its own, and it was never supposed to be; its job is speed, not size.

The rest of what Nepal is counting on is money that has been offered, not delivered. The Asian Development Bank moved fast with a $5 million emergency grant within two days of the disaster, and India, China, and the World Bank have together pledged roughly $229 million toward the government's relief fund.37

Beyond that, Nepal has formally asked the United Nations' global Loss and Damage Fund, the mechanism created specifically to help vulnerable countries pay for climate disasters, for an initial $20 million. But that fund is already badly backed up: it has received $822 million in pledges from 27 countries and partners, has actually paid out only about $485 million of that, and already had 176 other eligible claims worth $2.8 billion waiting in line before Nepal's request arrived, with none of them yet funded.38 Nepal's own Finance Minister Swarnim Wagle and Govinda Raj Pokharel, who ran the National Planning Commission's 2015 earthquake needs assessment, have both said grants alone, from any source, are unlikely to cover the full reconstruction cost, meaning some portion will have to come as loans regardless of how the compensation question is resolved.39 Add the Cat DDO, the ADB grant, the bilateral pledges, and Nepal's full Loss and Damage ask together, and the total still falls well short of $4.7 billion. None of these sources was built to close a gap this size on its own, and none of them, alone or combined, was ever designed to.

The Revolution

Nepal is not the first country to face this problem, and the same World Bank financial instrument Nepal is now leaning on has already been tested, repeatedly, by a country that treats it very differently.

Case Study 1: The Philippines, Redesigning Disaster Financing Every Few Years

In 2011, the Philippines became the first country in Asia and the Pacific to sign a Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Option, or Cat DDO, with the World Bank: a pre-arranged $500 million line of credit that a government can draw down immediately once it declares a state of calamity, rather than waiting to negotiate emergency financing after a disaster has already struck. That first facility was fully drawn down after Tropical Storm Sendong hit later that same year.40 The Philippines did not stop there. A second $500 million Cat DDO followed in December 2015 and was fully drawn down after Typhoon Mangkhut in 2018. In April 2020, facing the compounding pressure of COVID-19, the government asked the World Bank to restructure its next $500 million facility into a more flexible policy loan rather than a standard Cat DDO,41 then returned to the Cat DDO structure with a fourth $500 million facility approved in November 2021.

That fourth facility was tested within weeks. When Typhoon Rai, known locally as Odette, hit the Philippines in December 2021, President Rodrigo Duterte declared a State of National Calamity on December 21, and the World Bank released $80 million from the facility on December 28, seven days later.42 Four years later, the pattern repeated at full scale: Typhoon Kalmaegi, known locally as Tino, made landfall on November 4, 2025, the government declared a State of National Calamity on November 6, and the World Bank released the entire $500 million from what is now called the Disaster Risk Management and Climate Cat DDO on November 28, twenty-four days after landfall.43 Across five iterations and fourteen years, the Philippines has kept the facility at a stable, easy-to-underwrite $500 million and simply renewed, and where necessary restructured, it every few years, rather than renegotiating a new figure from first principles after each disaster. The facility is designed to revolve and renew up to four times over a maximum fifteen-year span, so the redesign is written into the instrument itself, not left to whichever government happens to be in office when it expires.44 The point is not that the Philippines avoids disasters. It is that the country decided once, structurally, how it would keep paying for them, and then let that decision run on its own schedule.

Case Study 2: Nepal's Own 2015 Earthquake, and Why It Adopted a Cat DDO

Nepal has the same type of instrument, and its own version of the disaster that shows why it needed one. The April 2015 earthquake killed nearly 9,000 people and caused an estimated $7 billion in damage and losses, but Nepal had no pre-arranged contingent financing line at the time.45 The National Reconstruction Authority, the body created to run the recovery, took almost nine months of political wrangling before a permanent chief executive was even appointed, and its leadership changed three times in the following year.46 Two years after the quake, barely 10 percent of the roughly 500,000 damaged homes had been rebuilt, despite $4.1 billion in pledged donor support.47 The World Bank's own retrospective on the episode is blunt: in the absence of a contingent financing line such as a Cat DDO, it took approximately 300 days after the earthquake for the first recovery and reconstruction financing to actually reach the government's treasury.48

That gap is exactly why Nepal signed its own Cat DDO with the World Bank in March 2020, starting at $50 million, and increased it to $150 million in October 2024, less than two years before the Bhote Koshi flood.49 Unlike the Philippines' facility, Nepal's has never been tied to a formula, a percentage of GDP, or a scheduled review; it has been adjusted twice in six years, each time through a fresh, standalone negotiation with the World Bank rather than an automatic renewal built into the instrument itself. Those funds are already being used following the August flood, alongside the ADB grant and bilateral pledges described above. This time, Nepal did not have to wait 300 days for money to start moving. But a disaster risk finance official has already warned that even a $150 million Cat DDO leaves a $95 to $245 million financing shortfall for a single ten-year flood-and-landslide event on its own, let alone a $4.7 billion reconstruction bill.50

CASE STUDY: Bhutan Paid to Lower a Lake Before It Could Burst

Between 2008 and 2012, the Royal Government of Bhutan, with financing from the Global Environment Facility and support from the United Nations Development Programme, sent teams of workers to Thorthormi Lake, one of the most dangerous glacial lakes in the country, for four summer field seasons at altitudes above 4,400 meters. Using hand tools and no heavy machinery, they manually lowered the lake's water level by roughly five meters to reduce pressure on its unstable moraine dam.51 The work was carried out through Bhutan's Department of Geology and Mines and its Disaster Management Division, standing government bodies rather than a commission created for the occasion, which is part of why the project survived four full field seasons without losing momentum to a change in political leadership. It is now cited as a benchmark example of community-based glacial lake outburst flood risk reduction in the Himalaya, and it cost a fraction of what a single major outburst flood would have. Nepal, sitting on a comparable cluster of high-risk lakes, has not undertaken anything at this scale.

The Solution

None of what follows fixes the immediate crisis. It is what would have to change, structurally and institutionally, for the next glacier collapse to look more like the Philippines' response and less like Nepal's own 2015 recovery.

Put the Cat DDO on a redesign cycle, not a one-off number

Nepal's $150 million facility, and even its theoretical $215 million ceiling under the World Bank's own GDP-based formula, will always be a rounding error against a multi-billion-dollar disaster. That is not a reason to abandon the instrument, since it is exactly what let Nepal start moving money in days rather than months after August 26. It is a reason to stop treating its size as a fixed number and start treating it the way the Philippines does: as a facility that gets revisited and resized on a standing schedule, written into the arrangement itself, rather than a figure Nepal has to renegotiate from scratch with the World Bank every few years depending on whichever government is in office. Layering in additional instruments, such as parametric insurance that pays out automatically against a measured trigger like river height or seismic signal, would add coverage the Cat DDO alone cannot provide, without requiring a political decision each time.

Build a standing climate resilience office, insulated from politics

Bhutan's Thorthormi lake-lowering project worked partly because it prevented a disaster rather than paying for one afterward, and partly because it sat inside permanent technical departments that outlasted any single government. Nepal's own 2015 recovery shows the opposite failure mode: the National Reconstruction Authority had to be invented from nothing after the earthquake, and its leadership changed three times while political coalitions shifted underneath it.52 Nepal's national government has changed twice in the past year, from the September 2025 protests that toppled the Oli government through to Prime Minister Balendra Shah's administration, which is now six months old and managing this disaster on top of the anti-corruption mandate it was elected on.

The fix is to stop rebuilding this capacity from zero every time. A permanent climate resilience office, housed inside the existing National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority rather than spun up as a new body after each disaster, could do two things Nepal currently lacks: identify and physically mitigate its own highest-risk glacial lakes the way Bhutan did, out of the roughly 200 candidates already flagged by regional scientists,53 and negotiate financing terms with the World Bank and the Global Environment Facility on standing technical relationships, rather than needing a fresh political mandate every time the government in Kathmandu changes. Housing it inside an existing statutory authority, instead of chartering a new commission after the next disaster, is what would let it survive the next change of government intact.

Rebuild hydropower away from the same river corridors, not back into them

Several of the thirteen hydropower projects damaged on August 26 sit on a stretch of river that had already flooded once in the preceding fourteen months,54 on top of the 32 projects hit elsewhere in the country during 2025.55 Nepal is planning to expand installed capacity from roughly 3,800 megawatts today to around 28,500 megawatts by the mid-2030s,56 and every one of those new projects is a decision about where, not just whether, to build. Siting and engineering standards for new hydropower and its transmission infrastructure should explicitly account for glacial hazard exposure upstream, rather than defaulting to the same narrow, already-proven river gorges that concentrate both the best hydropower sites and the worst flood risk in the same few kilometers of terrain.

Push the Loss and Damage Fund to actually pay out, not just pledge

Nepal's $20 million claim to the global Loss and Damage Fund is now a live test of whether that fund can move at anything close to the speed its own name implies.57 The fund has received more than $822 million in pledges from 27 countries and partners, but only about $485 million has actually been paid in, and 176 other eligible claims worth $2.8 billion were already pending before Nepal's request arrived, with none of them yet funded.58 Nepal's own Finance Minister Swarnim Wagle and Govinda Raj Pokharel, who ran the National Planning Commission's 2015 earthquake needs assessment, have both said grants alone, from any source, are unlikely to cover the full reconstruction cost, meaning some portion will have to come as loans regardless of how the compensation question is resolved.59

Closing

Nepal did not repeat every mistake of 2015. A Cat DDO facility, an ADB grant approved within two days, and swift bilateral pledges meant that money started moving in days rather than months. But a $150 million contingent line against a $4.7 billion bill was never going to be a financing plan on its own, it is a down payment, and a country whose government has changed twice in the past year cannot afford to keep rebuilding its disaster-preparedness capacity from zero every time.

The Philippines shows what it looks like to write the redesign into the financial instrument itself, so a facility keeps working no matter who is in office when the next typhoon makes landfall. Bhutan shows that the same principle applies to prevention: a permanent technical office, not a commission stood up after the fact, is what let a lake-lowering project survive four full field seasons intact. Nepal now has the chance to combine both lessons before the next glacier gives way, rather than after, and it can do that while its compensation claim against India, China, and the United States works its way through the UN system on its own timeline. Those two tracks do not have to wait on each other, whatever the politics around either one eventually decide.

Early Warning Signs, for Any Country

A contingent financing facility with no redesign cycle: a Cat DDO or similar instrument that has been touched once or twice on an ad hoc basis, rather than renewed on a standing schedule written into the arrangement itself, will quietly fall behind the risk it was built for.

Disaster-preparedness capacity that gets rebuilt from zero after every event: if a country would have to invent the institution before it can even start spending pledged money, the recovery is already starting months behind.

A known, mapped hazard cluster with no standing mitigation program: repeated events on the same river system, as Nepal saw on the Bhote Koshi in 2025 and again in 2026, are a warning that is easy to document and easy to ignore.

Reliance on ad hoc donor pledges rather than pre-arranged instruments: pledges announced at a donor conference, or claims filed with an international fund, are not the same as money that can be drawn down on a fixed timeline.

Critical infrastructure concentrated in the same narrow, already-proven corridors: when the best sites for a given industry and the worst sites for a given hazard overlap, rebuilding in place after a disaster recreates the exposure rather than reducing it.
