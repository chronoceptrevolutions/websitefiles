Summary

In late 2025, a woman in Mumbai got out of her car, walked into a political rally blocking a busy road, and confronted a sitting cabinet minister over the traffic jam that had kept her from reaching her child's school bus. The video went viral within hours, and it split the country between those who called her rude and those who called her a hero. But the underlying problem is bigger than one video: VIP convoys routinely stop traffic, including ambulances, for officials who are not legally entitled to that privilege, at a direct cost to ordinary people's safety, time, and money. This is not unique to India. It shows up from South Africa to the United States. What is unique is how deeply the culture of deference to authority is embedded in India, and what it would take to change it.

Problem

VIP convoys are not unique to India

Earlier in 2026, South African Deputy President Paul Mashatile's official convoy was repeatedly filmed driving against oncoming traffic during the morning rush in Midrand, allegedly blocking oncoming lanes along Allandale Drive near the Mall of Africa before proceeding on the wrong side of the road, footage residents said showed a pattern rather than a one-off.1

Even the world's most powerful democracy is not immune. During the September 2025 UN General Assembly in New York, President Trump's motorcade was given such priority that France's President Macron, Turkey's President Erdogan, and South Korea's President Lee Jae-myung were each separately stopped by police and left waiting on the street until it passed. Macron called Trump directly from the sidewalk to say he was stuck.2

So whether it is Nepal, Bangladesh, Brazil, or anywhere else, VIP culture shows up in some form nearly everywhere power exists. The difference is in how normalized it has become, and how it is dealt with when it is exposed.

The Worli traffic incident

The video that reignited this conversation in India shows the BJP's "Mahila Jan Aakrosh" rally, organized by the Mahayuti alliance's women's wing over the Women's Reservation Bill, on a Tuesday afternoon on Annie Besant Road in Worli, Mumbai, timed close to school dismissal hours. Teena Choudhry, a mother on her way to pick up her child, was stuck in the resulting jam for over an hour, watching her child's school bus pass by without being able to reach it. She got out of her car, walked into the rally, found Maharashtra minister Girish Mahajan, and confronted him directly over the traffic chaos, then turned on the police when they tried to intervene.3

The video was viewed millions of times within hours and split public opinion. Some criticized her language and a reported instance of a water bottle being thrown; many more called her the voice of every commuter who has been stuck behind a VIP convoy and said nothing. Mahajan later apologized for the jam while criticizing her language, and said two buses were moved on his instructions so traffic could take a U-turn onto the main road, though only after the confrontation had already gone viral.3

What it costs: the golden hour

The World Health Organization's "golden hour" is the window in which emergency medical intervention can prevent death or permanent disability. That window is already dangerously narrow in Indian cities: ambulance speeds during peak hours in metropolitan traffic average roughly 8 to 12 kilometers per hour, well below walking-adjacent bicycle speed, and only about a fifth of trauma patients nationally reach a hospital within the golden hour at all.4

Despite that, an RTI application filed by activist Deepak Sharma and answered by the Ministry of Home Affairs confirmed that, under the MHA's own guidelines, traffic is legally supposed to be stopped only for the President, the Vice President, the Prime Minister, and visiting foreign dignitaries with equivalent security cover. Every other instance of traffic being stopped for a state minister, or someone further down the chain, falls outside that guideline.5

The Legal Reality

This is one of the rarer cases where the problem is not a missing law. The framework to protect ordinary commuters already exists. It is simply not enforced.

Constitutional rights and balance

Article 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(b) of the Indian Constitution guarantee the right to freedom of speech and the right to assemble peacefully without arms. But the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that this right is not absolute: it must be balanced against the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21, and against the equivalent rights of everyone else who is also trying to use public space.6

Supreme Court rulings

In the 2020 Shaheen Bagh case, dealing with protesters occupying a public road, a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court ruled: "Democracy and dissent go hand in hand, but then the demonstrations expressing dissent have to be in designated places alone." The Court added that blockage of a public way causes grave inconvenience to commuters, and that no single fundamental right exists in isolation, it has to be balanced against every other competing right.7

That reasoning was not invented for protesters alone. In the 1973 case Himat Lal K. Shah v. Commissioner of Police, Ahmedabad, the Supreme Court struck down a police rule requiring prior, unreasoned permission for public meetings, holding that officials cannot have uncanalised discretion to simply refuse. Later, in the 2012 Ramlila Maidan case, the Court held that police permission for rallies and processions is legally required, but that a refusal or withdrawal of that permission is only valid for exceptional reasons.8

The same logic that applies to a protest blocking a road applies to a political rally blocking a road. The law does not carve out an exception for VIPs.

The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita

Blocking a public road is, on paper, already a crime. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), which replaced the Indian Penal Code (IPC) on July 1, 2024, covers it in three places. Section 270 (replacing IPC Section 268) defines public nuisance as any act that causes common injury, danger, or annoyance to the public, and explicitly states that a nuisance is not excused just because it also serves some convenience or advantage, meaning good intentions are not a legal defense for the disruption caused.9

Section 285 (replacing IPC Section 283) covers causing danger or obstruction to any person in a public way, with the fine raised to up to 5,000 rupees, from just 200 rupees under the old IPC.10

Section 292 (replacing IPC Section 290) is the catch-all for public nuisance not otherwise covered, punishable by a fine of up to 1,000 rupees.11

Even after being raised from the colonial-era 200 rupee cap, a maximum fine in the low thousands of rupees is not a serious deterrent for a political rally, which is a large part of why these provisions are rarely enforced against anyone with real power.

The red beacon ban

In 2017, the government banned the red beacon, or lal batti, from every vehicle except genuine emergency vehicles, police, army, fire trucks, and ambulances. No union minister, chief minister, or state minister was allowed to keep one. Announcing the decision, Prime Minister Modi said, "Every Indian is special. Every Indian is a VIP," and called the beacon a symbol out of touch with the spirit of new India.12

Nine years later, the beacon is gone, but the culture it represented has not gone with it. A rally organized by the same ruling party was still able to shut down one of Mumbai's busiest streets for over an hour.

How Other Countries Handle This

Australia's bushfire reckoning

Australia is a useful case precisely because it shows what happens when a public servant gets this wrong, not because it is a flawless model. During the catastrophic 2019-2020 Black Summer bushfires, which would go on to burn roughly 12 million hectares, kill 33 people, and kill or injure over a billion animals, Prime Minister Scott Morrison left the country for a family holiday in Hawaii. His office did not announce it. He was only confirmed to be there after an Australian tourist recognized and photographed him at a resort.13

He cut the trip short and returned under pressure, then visited the fire-devastated town of Cobargo, New South Wales. There, Zoey Salucci-McDermott, a volunteer firefighter who was 28 weeks pregnant and had lost her home days earlier, refused to shake his hand unless he committed more funding to the Rural Fire Service. Morrison shook her hand anyway, said only "I understand," and moved on.14

The backlash was not confined to his political opponents; members of his own party publicly said they had not been told he was travelling. The nickname that followed him for the rest of his time in office, "Scotty from Marketing," reflected a durable public judgment: in Australia, being seen to abandon duty during a crisis has a real and lasting political cost.

Denmark, Sweden, and New Zealand

In Denmark, cycling to parliament is normal practice for national politicians, including prime ministers, with no convoy and no blocked roads required.15

In Sweden, ministers and members of parliament do not have official cars or personal drivers by default; only the prime minister has standing access to a security-force vehicle. Everyone else, including cabinet ministers, is expected to use public transport, and a politician spending public money on a taxi instead of the train has historically been enough to make headlines.16

In New Zealand, then-Prime Minister Jacinda Ardern gave birth while in office in 2018, becoming only the second elected head of government in modern history to do so, and the first to formally take maternity leave, returning after six weeks. When the Christchurch mosque shootings killed 51 people in March 2019, she was visibly present with affected families in the immediate aftermath, and the government banned military-style semi-automatic weapons within six days of the attack.17

None of these countries are free of political dysfunction. But in each case, the operating assumption is the same: holding office is a form of service, not a license to inconvenience the public that pays for it.

The Solution

This is the uncomfortable part, because the culture that allows VIP privilege to persist is not only built by politicians. It is also maintained by everyone else.

Stop treating politicians like celebrities. Courtesy and accountability are not contradictory. Respect the office without garlanding the person, and question the performance regardless of who holds the seat.

Learn and use RTI rights. Filing an RTI asking a local municipality how many rally permits were issued in a given month, whether a traffic management plan was submitted and approved, and who signed off on it, is free, and it turns a passive observer into an active participant.

Demand media accountability. Whether a story is framed as "brave commuter stands up to a minister" or "rude woman disrupts a rally" signals who a given outlet believes public office is supposed to serve.

Formalize public questioning. At town halls and constituency meetings, ask specific, uncomfortable questions, about unspent drainage budgets, about why rallies are scheduled during rush hour, rather than deferential ones.

Vote on performance, not identity. As long as elections are decided by caste, religion, or communal identity rather than delivery, elected officials have little incentive to perform, only to belong to the right group. This is the hardest of the five to shift, and the one that makes all the others matter.

Closing

None of the legal tools described above are new or imported. Article 19 and Article 21, the Shaheen Bagh and Ramlila Maidan rulings, and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita's public nuisance provisions already exist and already apply equally to a political rally and a protest blocking the same road. What is missing is enforcement, and a public willing to demand it consistently rather than only when a video goes viral.

The woman in Worli did not change the law. She used it, in spirit, the moment she said "first solve the traffic, then talk to me." The five steps above are what it looks like to do that consistently, not just once, and not just when it is caught on camera.
